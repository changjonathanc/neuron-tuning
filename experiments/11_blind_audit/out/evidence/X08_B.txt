## 1. The Primary Tool: One-Way ANOVA

If you have **one independent variable** (the treatment group, intervention type, etc.) with **three or more independent groups**, the **One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)** is the foundational test you should use.

### What it does:
* **Hypothesis Testing:** It tests the null hypothesis ($H_0$) that the means of all groups are equal ($\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3 = \dots$) against the alternative hypothesis ($H_a$) that at least one group mean is different.
* **Output:** It produces an **F-statistic** and a **p-value**.
    * **Low p-value (typically < 0.05):** Indicates that there is a statistically significant difference *somewhere* among the group means.
    * **High p-value:** Indicates that you do not have enough evidence to say the group means are different.

### ⚠️ The Limitation of ANOVA:
ANOVA only tells you **IF** a difference exists. It does **not** tell you **WHICH** specific pairs of groups are different (e.g., is Group A different from B, or B different from C?). If the ANOVA is significant, you need **post-hoc tests**.

---

## 2. Post-Hoc Tests (When ANOVA is Significant)

If your One-Way ANOVA yields a significant result (rejecting $H_0$), you must follow up with a **post-hoc test** to perform pairwise comparisons while controlling for the increased risk of Type I errors (false positives) that come from running multiple tests.

The choice of post-hoc test depends on your assumptions (especially normality and homogeneity of variances) and the structure of your data.

### A. Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD)
* **Best For:** Comparing **all possible pairs** of means when you have equal sample sizes across all groups and you want a family-wise error rate control.
* **Pros:** Very popular, robust, and controls the overall error rate well.
* **Cons:** Can be overly conservative if sample sizes are very unequal.

### B. Bonferroni Correction
* **Best For:** A very conservative approach, especially when you have a very small number of comparisons or very strict error control is needed.
* **How it works:** It adjusts the significance level ($\alpha$) for each individual test.
* **Pros:** Very safe against false positives.
* **Cons:** Very conservative; increases the chance of missing a true difference (Type II error).

### C. Games-Howell Test
* **Best For:** When the assumption of **homogeneity of variances** (equal variances across groups) is violated.
* **Pros:** Robust when variances are unequal.
* **Cons:** Less powerful than Tukey's if variances *are* actually equal.
